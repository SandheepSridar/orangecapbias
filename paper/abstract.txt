The Orange Cap is the most sought after individual batting award in the IPL. In this paper we discuss how it systemically disadvantages middle order batsmen and the high performers who don't qualify for play-offs. This bias stems from three advantages: greater ball-faced volume, exclusive access to power play conditions, and additional match opportunities from play-off qualification.

Using ball-by-ball data from cricsheet with 295,557 deliveries across 19 seasons, we show that power play access, play-off matches are strong determinants of the Orange Cap award. We are not comparing players or evaluating player performance,  we are assessing the fairness of the award's design.

The results from the analysis confirm each hypothesis. We found that the openers face a median of 71 more balls than a middle-order batsman and openers score 57% of their runs in the power play overs. When league runs are normalized to a 14 match baseline — projecting players up to a full season only when their team missed the play-offs, so that a player's own absence is not credited against batters who played the full league — 74.1% of the batters per season rank differently confirming the advantage of match-access. Players qualified for the play-offs play a median of two additional matches per season increasing the margin for the Orange Cap. In all 19 seasons every Orange Cap winner has batted in positions 1-3.

The findings demonstrate that match access determines the award outcome and not batting skill alone. The award structurally disincentivizes the middle-order batting skill. Beyond the unfairness in individual recognition, this has broader implications for the well-documented importance of number 4 position at the international level. We call for a re-evaluation of the Orange Cap criteria to account for structural positional and match-count advantages.
