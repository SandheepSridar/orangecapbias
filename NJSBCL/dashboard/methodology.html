Methodology

Every metric on this dashboard, how it's actually calculated, and a small worked example with made-up round numbers so the arithmetic is easy to follow. Source: build_data.py (server-side, run once per rescrape) unless noted as client-side.

The basics — the building blocks everything else uses

Four numbers that show up almost everywhere else on this page, so they're covered once here instead of re-explained each time.

batting average = total runs ÷ times out (not-outs don't count as an "out") strike rate = 100 × total runs ÷ balls faced economy rate = runs conceded ÷ overs bowled dot-ball % = 100 × dot balls ÷ legal balls bowled
Example

Batting: a player scores 20, 35*, 10, 45 across 4 innings (the * means not out). They were out 3 times for a total of 110 runs, so average = 110 ÷ 3 = 36.7. If those 4 innings took 80 balls total, strike rate = 100 × 110 ÷ 80 = 137.5.

Bowling: a bowler concedes 24 runs in 4 overs → economy = 24 ÷ 4 = 6.0. If 14 of the 24 legal balls in that spell went for zero runs, dot-ball % = 100 × 14 ÷ 24 = 58.3%.

Comparing players fairly — z-scores

Several rankings below (bowling strengths/weaknesses, batting/bowling quality for the Best XI, bowler-by-phase) judge a player against the whole league rather than just their own teammates, so "our best bowler" actually means something. A z-score says how many standard deviations above or below the league average a number is.

z = (player's value − league average) ÷ league standard deviation
Example

Across the whole league, bowling economy averages 6.0 with a standard deviation of 1.2. A bowler with economy 4.8 has z = (4.8 − 6.0) ÷ 1.2 = −1.0 — a full standard deviation better than average (lower economy is better, which is why economy's z-score gets flipped/negated before being combined with other stats below — a more negative raw economy z needs to count as a positive contribution).

Composite scores (like "strengthScore" or "battingScore" below) are just the average of two or three z-scores — each stat contributes equally, none dominates just because it happens to be measured in bigger numbers.

Win probability — Elo rating

Same rating system chess uses. Every team starts the season at 1500. After each completed match, both teams' ratings move based on the result and how surprising it was — beating a stronger team gains more than beating a weaker one. Processed in chronological order (match ID order) across every match in the season, not just ours.

expected win% for A = 1 ÷ (1 + 10^((Elo_B − Elo_A) ÷ 400)) actual result = 1 for a win, 0 for a loss, 0.5 for a tie new Elo_A = old Elo_A + K × (actual − expected) [K = 32]

The win-probability number shown on the matchup page is just this same "expected win%" formula applied to both teams' current ratings — it doesn't know about toss, weather, or who's actually available to play.

Example

Team A is rated 1550, Team B 1500. Expected win% for A = 1 ÷ (1 + 10^((1500−1550)÷400)) = 1 ÷ (1 + 10^−0.125) ≈ 57.7%. Say A wins: new Elo_A = 1550 + 32 × (1 − 0.577) ≈ 1564. Team B drops by the same amount it "should" have — new Elo_B = 1500 + 32 × (0 − 0.423) ≈ 1487.

League points system — standings page

Not computed by this dashboard — the standings page shows NJSBCL's own official points table exactly as scraped from cricclubs.com. Included here for reference, straight from the league rule book (sections 4 and 52):

win = 4 points tie (same score) or no result = 2 points each loss = 0 points bonus point = +1 for the winning team if their run rate was at least 1.25× the losing team's run rate forfeit = 0 points to the forfeiting team, 4 to the opponent

Ties (final scores level) and no-results (washed-out/abandoned matches) both split 2 points each, but the standings table tracks them in separate T and NR columns since they're different situations. Ranking within a group goes by points first, then head-to-head record, then net run rate (NRR) as the tiebreaker — see the rule book for the full tiebreaker order.

Toss advice — bat first or bowl first?

Compares a team's win% batting first vs win% chasing this season (needs at least 3 completed matches on each side). If one is at least 10 percentage points worse than the other, that's the recommendation; otherwise it's a coin flip either way.

if chasing win% ≤ batting-first win% − 10pp → recommend "bat first" if batting-first win% ≤ chasing win% − 10pp → recommend "bowl first" otherwise → "no strong lean, pick on conditions"
Example

A team has won 7 of 10 matches batting first (70%) but only 4 of 10 chasing (40%). The gap is 30 percentage points — well past the 10pp threshold — so the recommendation is "bat first, force them to chase."

Chase-pacing calculator — client-side, matchup page

The only calculator on this list that runs entirely in your browser (app.js's renderChasePlan()), recomputing live as you type a target. It splits a chase into four 4-over blocks and assumes the fielding team's best bowlers (lowest economy, "bowling strengths") open and close the innings while their weakest ("weak bowlers") cover the middle — a standard captaincy pattern. Each block's share of the target is weighted by the block's assumed economy, so a tougher block asks for fewer runs.

block economies = [strong, weak, weak, strong] (their average econ in each pool) block target = round(total target × block econ ÷ sum of all 4 econs) (any 1-2 run rounding drift is absorbed into the last block, so the four always sum to the target)
Example

Target = 120. Their strong bowlers average econ 5, weak bowlers average econ 8 → block econs = [5, 8, 8, 5], summing to 26. Block 1 = round(120 × 5 ÷ 26) = 23. Block 2 = round(120 × 8 ÷ 26) = 37. Block 3 = 37. Block 4 = 23. Total = 23 + 37 + 37 + 23 = 120 — matches the target exactly.

Home / away record — season record grid

Win% split by venue. Venue is worked out from the schedule export's "Ground" field, which is reliably just the hosting team's own name — matched to each completed match by team-pair and chronological order (the schedule and results tables don't share a match ID).

home win% = 100 × home wins ÷ home matches played away win% = 100 × away wins ÷ away matches played
Example

A team plays 8 matches at home (5 wins) and 6 away (2 wins). Home win% = 100 × 5 ÷ 8 = 63%. Away win% = 100 × 2 ÷ 6 = 33%.

Batting collapses — how often does the innings fall apart?

Looks only at the top 7 batters in each innings (scorecard row order = batting/arrival order — the tail folding cheaply at the end of a 10-11 batter innings is normal, not a "collapse"). Finds the longest run of 3 or more consecutive dismissals whose combined runs add up to 20 or fewer. If one exists, that innings counts as a collapse; the biggest qualifying stretch (most wickets, ties broken by fewest runs) is flagged as that innings' worst instance.

collapse% = 100 × innings with a qualifying stretch ÷ total innings
Example

Top-7 scores in one innings: 40, 5, 0, 3, 30, 2, 15. The 5, 0, 3 stretch is 3 consecutive wickets for 5 + 0 + 3 = 8 runs — under the 20-run cap, so this innings counts as a collapse, flagged as "3 wickets for 8 runs."

Recent form — hot / cold / steady

Compares a batter's average over their last 5 innings to their season average. Needs at least 3 innings total to render a verdict at all.

ratio = (last-5-innings average) ÷ (season average) ratio ≥ 1.25 → "hot" ratio ≤ 0.75 → "cold" otherwise → "steady"
Example

Season average = 20 runs/innings. Average across the last 5 innings = 30. Ratio = 30 ÷ 20 = 1.5, past the 1.25 threshold → tagged "hot."

Top-scorer dependence — how much do they lean on one player?

What share of a team's top-3 run-scorers' combined runs this season came from their #1 scorer alone. Higher means the batting is more concentrated in one player — containing or removing him early matters more.

dependence % = 100 × top scorer's runs ÷ (top scorer's + #2's + #3's runs)
Example

A team's top 3 run-scorers have 400, 250, and 150 runs this season (800 combined). Dependence = 100 × 400 ÷ 800 = 50% — half of their top order's output comes from one player.

Key-batsman win impact — "what if we get him out early?"

Splits a specific batter's innings into "scored at-or-above his own season median" vs "scored below it" (needs 3+ matches on each side), then compares the team's win% in matches from each bucket. Low-score innings are the closest proxy available for an early dismissal — there's no per-match dismissal-timing data for opponent teams, only final runs scored.

swing = (team win% when he scores ≥ his median) − (team win% when he scores < his median)
Example

His season median score is 25. In the 5 matches he scored 25+, the team won 4 (80%). In the 5 matches he scored under 25, the team won 1 (20%). Swing = 80 − 20 = +60 percentage points.

Dismissal & wicket-type breakdowns — how do they get out / take wickets?

For a batter: what % of their dismissals this season were each type (Bowled, Caught, LBW, Run Out, …). For a bowler: same idea, but the types of wickets they've taken. Not-outs are excluded from the batter's total.

% for a dismissal type = 100 × count of that type ÷ total dismissals
Example

A batter has been dismissed 10 times this season: 5 Caught, 3 Bowled, 2 LBW → Caught 50%, Bowled 30%, LBW 20%.

Bowling battle — strengths & weaknesses

Two mirrored rankings, both z-scored against the whole league (min 8 overs bowled to qualify for either).

Bowling strengths (our best, to build the attack around): low economy plus a high dot-ball rate.

strengthScore = ( z(dot-ball %) − z(economy) ) ÷ 2

Weak bowlers to target (opponent's weakest): poor economy, a bad single-match spell, and extras (wides/no-balls) leaked.

weaknessScore = ( z(economy) + z(worst single-match economy) + z(extras rate) ) ÷ 3
Example (bowling strengths)

League-wide, economy averages 6.5 (std dev 1.0) and dot-ball % averages 45% (std dev 10%). Bowler A has economy 5.0 and dot-ball % 60%. Economy z = (5.0 − 6.5) ÷ 1.0 = −1.5, negated to +1.5. Dot-ball z = (60 − 45) ÷ 10 = +1.5. strengthScore = (1.5 + 1.5) ÷ 2 = +1.5 — a full 1.5 standard deviations better than league average on both counts.

Bowler by phase — who's best in which 4-over block?

Splits the season into four 4-over blocks of a 16-over innings (overs 1-4/5-8/9-12/13-16) and ranks our own bowlers within each block. Needs ball-by-ball detail (not just over totals) to compute dot-ball %, wicket rate, and extras rate — min 3 overs bowled in that specific block to qualify. Z-scored against only the other bowlers who bowled in that same block, not the whole season.

wicket rate = wickets ÷ overs bowled (in that block) extras rate = (wides + no-balls) ÷ overs bowled (in that block) phaseScore = ( z(dot%) + z(wicket rate) − z(extras rate) ) ÷ 3
Example

Two bowlers bowled overs 13-16 this season. Across just those two, dot% averages 60% (std dev 10%) and wicket rate averages 0.8/over (std dev 0.4). Bowler A: dot 70%, wicket rate 1.2/over, extras rate 0 → dot z = +1.0, wicket-rate z = +1.0, extras z = 0 (say the league average extras rate here is also 0) → phaseScore = (1.0 + 1.0 − 0) ÷ 3 ≈ +0.67, ranking above Bowler B.

Death overs (last 3) — who do we trust under pressure?

Narrower and simpler than bowler-by-phase: looks only at the literal last 3 overs of each innings we bowled (e.g. overs 12-14 of a 14-over innings, not a fixed "overs 13-16" range), and ranks purely by economy — no dot%/wicket-rate/extras composite. Min 3 such overs bowled to qualify.

economy (death overs) = runs conceded in the last 3 overs ÷ overs bowled in the last 3 overs
Example

A bowler has bowled 5 "death overs" across the season for a combined 28 runs → economy = 28 ÷ 5 = 5.6. Lower is better; bowlers are ranked lowest economy first.

Boundary dependence — how much comes from fours and sixes?

boundary dependence % = 100 × (fours × 4 + sixes × 6) ÷ total runs scored
Example

A team scores 600 runs across the season, including 40 fours and 20 sixes. Boundary runs = 40 × 4 + 20 × 6 = 280. Boundary dependence = 100 × 280 ÷ 600 = 46.7%.

Par score to set & target to chase — matchup-specific numbers

Par score to set: the opponent's average score, across every match this season they lost (whether they batted first or second) — roughly "this is what it takes to beat them." Target to chase: their own average score when they batted first — a read on what to expect if we win the toss and bowl. Both need at least 3 qualifying matches.

par score to set = average(opponent's score) across their losses this season target to chase = average(opponent's score) across matches they batted first
Example

An opponent has lost 4 matches this season, conceding scores of 145, 160, 150, and 165. Par score to set = (145 + 160 + 150 + 165) ÷ 4 = 155.

Head-to-head — just a tally

No formula here — just every completed match between us and this specific opponent this season, with the result and both scores, plus a wins/losses/ties count.

Win dependency — who do we depend on to win?

Role-agnostic version of the key-batsman idea above, run for every player (batting or bowling) with 6+ matches. Splits their games into a "good half" and "bad half" against their own season median, then compares team win% in each. Only players with a positive swing are kept, top 6 by swing. Two extra floors keep this from surfacing noise: batters need a season-median of at least 10 runs (otherwise "good half" just means "batted longer, not better"), and bowlers need to average at least 2 overs a match (so one-over cameos don't swing wildly on a tiny sample).

batting: good half = innings with runs ≥ his own season median bowling: good half = spells with economy ≤ his own season median (lower economy = better) swing = (team win% in good half) − (team win% in bad half)
Example

A batter's season median is 18 runs. In matches he scored 18+, the team won 6 of 8 (75%). In matches he scored under 18, the team won 2 of 8 (25%). Swing = 75 − 25 = +50 percentage points.

Best XI selection — battingScore, bowlingScore, and the picking order

Two more z-scored-against-the-whole-league composites, same pattern as the bowling battle above.

battingScore = ( z(batting average) + z(strike rate) ) ÷ 2 [min 5 innings] bowlingScore = ( z(dot-ball %) − z(economy) ) ÷ 2 [min 8 overs — same formula as strengthScore]

The XI itself is picked in a fixed order: (1) the wicketkeeper (identified from the scorecard's † marker) is auto-picked; (2) fill up to 6 batting spots by battingScore, best first; (3) add bowling options by bowlingScore until 5 recognized bowlers are in the XI; (4) fill any remaining spots by combined value (the sum of whichever scores a player has — someone with no qualifying score at all ranks dead last, not "neutral," so an unproven player only gets picked once every real option is exhausted).

Example

Four candidates for the last batting spot, ranked by battingScore: Player A (+0.8), Player B (+0.1), Player C (no qualifying innings yet), Player D (−0.3). Picking order is A, then B, then D — Player C, despite having "no negative score," is treated as worse than even D's below-average +(−0.3) and picked last, as a fallback only.

AI insights — top 3 things worth changing

Three fixed checks, each comparing "who the numbers say is best" against "who's actually getting the overs or the batting position." Each one returns nothing if there's no real gap to report — the list is never padded to force exactly 3 insights.

1. Phase-bowling mismatch: across the four bowler-by-phase blocks, the biggest gap between a block's top-phaseScore bowler and whoever actually bowled the most overs there.

2. Batting-order mismatch: the batter with the biggest gap between their battingScore quality-rank (1 = best in the squad) and their actual average batting position this season (from scorecard row order).

3. Season-wide bowling workload mismatch: our best bowler by strengthScore vs whoever's actually bowled the most overs overall, when they differ and the latter has bowled more.

batting-order gap = actual average batting position − quality rank (1 = best battingScore) (the batter with the single largest positive gap is the one surfaced — quality far outstripping where they actually bat in the order)
Example

A squad's battingScore ranking (best first): #1 Player A, #2 Player B, #3 Player C. Their actual average batting positions this season are 3.5, 5.4, and 2.0. Gaps: A = 3.5 − 1 = 2.5; B = 5.4 − 2 = 3.4; C = 2.0 − 3 = −1.0. Player B has the biggest gap → the insight reads "Move Player B up the batting order."

Right/wrong verdicts — last-match recap

After each completed match, six checks (five of which have a "wrong" mirror — the sixth only ever reports a hit, see #6) compare a pre-match read the dashboard already surfaces elsewhere against what actually happened. Each check only returns a verdict when the evidence is reasonably clear-cut (a fixed margin, not a coin flip) — a quiet match can come back with fewer than 3 in either bucket rather than being padded to fill a quota. All checks use full-season stats (which include the match just played, since removing one match out of 15-20 barely moves a season average).

Deliberately excludes squad selection ("did our top-rated picks even play today") from right/wrong — who's actually available and picked is volatile and not a strategic call this recap should second-guess. Every check below only evaluates how the players who actually took the field performed, not who they were.

1. Format read: did we bat/bowl first as the toss-advice recommendation said, and did we win? 2. Par score: batting first, did we score at or near the par score to set (within 5 runs right, 15+ short is wrong)? Bowling first, did we hold them near/below their usual first-innings total? 3. Key batsman: did their #1 run-scorer finish above or below his own "tipping point" threshold? 4. Bowling strengths: for whichever of our top economy/dot-rate bowlers actually bowled, did their economy today back up their season number (within 1.0/over) or blow well past it? 5. Weak bowlers: when one of their flagged weakest bowlers actually bowled, did we punish him (economy 1.0+/over worse than his season rate) or let him off (1.0+/over better)? 6. Bowling weaknesses, broader net: whoever conceded the highest economy for the opposition this match (min 2 overs) — if he wasn't already one of the 3 explicitly flagged weakest bowlers, this only speaks up if his season economy independently sits above the league average too, so a single expensive day doesn't get misread as a season- long pattern that isn't actually there. When it fires, also folds in team-wide extras (wides/no-balls) if notably high next to their own season rate.

Example

Toss advice for an opponent says "bat first" (they win only 35% chasing vs 70% setting a total). We bat first today and win → right, "read the format right." If instead we bat first and lose, or we bowl first (going against the read) — no verdict either way, since the evidence doesn't cleanly confirm or contradict the model.

AI insight follow-through — did we act on it, and what happened?

For each of this season's (up to 3) AI insights, checks whether it was actionable this match (the player it's about actually took the field) and, if so, whether the recommendation was followed and what happened. Kept separate from the right/wrong verdicts above on purpose — the outcome here is often genuinely mixed ("followed, but didn't pay off today"), which a clean hit/miss verdict can't represent honestly. Each of the 3 insight types is checked differently:

Batting-order insight ("Move X up the order"): not actionable if X didn't bat. If he did, followed = his batting position this match is at least half a spot higher than his season-average position. Outcome reads off his actual score — 20+ counts as paying off; anything less is reported as the correct call with a bad individual result (plus a note that it cost nothing, when we still won). Phase-bowling insight ("Bowl X more in overs Y-Z"): not actionable if X didn't bowl at all; followed = he bowled at least one over specifically inside that phase this match (checked against the same ball-by-ball data bowler-by-phase uses). Bowling-workload insight ("Bowl X more overall"): not actionable if X didn't bowl; followed = he bowled 3+ overs (a near-full allotment of the 4-over max).

Example

The season's AI insights include "Move Harshad up the batting order" (season-average position 5.5). Today he batted at #2 — well up from 5.5, so followed = true — but was out for a 3-ball duck. Since we still won the match, the follow-through note reads: "He batted at #2 (up from his 5.5 season-average position, matching our insight), but was out for a 3-ball duck. Correct process, bad individual outcome — though it cost nothing given the final margin."

Star of the Match — a disclosed, bias-checked formula, not a judgment call

Batting and bowling only — fielding (catches, run-outs, stumpings) isn't credited, since fielder names aren't extracted from dismissal text anywhere in this pipeline. Every player who batted or bowled gets an impact score; highest wins. The full score table for every player is shown alongside the pick, so the ranking is always checkable, not just asserted.

Raw points, then z-scored — not raw points alone. An earlier version of this ranked players on raw points directly, and it was biased: wickets are worth 20 points each and dot balls add up fast, so a routine bowling spell scored far more than a routine batting innings just from the discipline's own arithmetic, not because it was actually the better performance. Checked against every batting/bowling row this season (2026-08-16): median bowling spell scored ~32-35 raw points vs ~3-5 for a median batting innings — a ~7-10x gap that let a modest 3-wicket haul routinely outrank a well-struck fifty, and compounded further for all-rounders, who stacked both scales. The fix: z-score each discipline's raw points against its own league-wide distribution before combining — same pattern this dashboard already uses everywhere else two different units need a fair comparison (bowling strengths, Best XI, bowler-by-phase) — so a performance is judged against a normal day in the same discipline, not against the other one's bigger numbers.

batting points = runs + (4s × 1) + (6s × 2) + SR bonus [SR bonus only if 10+ balls faced] SR ≥ 150 → +10 | SR ≥ 125 → +5 | SR < 60 → −5 bowling points = (wickets × 20) + (dot balls × 1) + economy bonus [econ bonus only if 2+ overs bowled] econ ≤ 4 → +10 | econ ≤ 6 → +5 | econ ≥ 10 → −10 | econ ≥ 8 → −5 battingZ = (batting points − league-wide mean batting points) ÷ league-wide std dev bowlingZ = (bowling points − league-wide mean bowling points) ÷ league-wide std dev impact score = battingZ + bowlingZ (whichever apply — 0 contribution from a discipline not played)
Example

A bowler returns 4/8 off 4 overs (17 dot balls, econ 2.0) — bowling points = (4 × 20) + 17 + 10 (econ ≤ 4 bonus) = 107. Against a league-wide bowling mean of ~36 and std dev of ~31 this season, bowlingZ = (107 − 36) ÷ 31 ≈ +2.31. He also scored 12 off 9 balls (1 four, 1 six) — under the 10-ball minimum for a strike-rate bonus, so batting points = 12 + 1 + 2 = 15; against a batting mean of ~10 and std dev of ~16, battingZ = (15 − 10) ÷ 16 ≈ +0.30. Impact score = 2.31 + 0.30 = +2.61. A teammate who instead scored an unbeaten 67 off 30 balls with big boundary hitting (67 raw batting points) would score battingZ = (67 − 10) ÷ 16 ≈ +3.59 — now correctly ranking above the bowling performance, which the old raw-points version would have gotten backwards.
